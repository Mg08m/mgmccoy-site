Chapter 1 The Rot

The complete first chapter of Kept Ground by MG McCoy, as published. About 2,400 words; ten minutes to read. No email, no sign-up.

The bolt went soft in her hand.

Not warm-soft. Fruit-soft. Her thumb kept going. Sal swore, quiet, and wiped the grey paste down her thigh. Wet ash. Battery left out in the rain.

Below the grate, the pit was still going. Two lads in scabbed harness, one of them already listing because his left knee had started to eat itself. The crowd didn’t mind. They never did, so long as somebody stayed upright long enough to bet on.

“Ward.” Donal’s voice, from the shadow by the pump housing. “If that lamp drops on my floor I will take it out of your neck.”

“It’s not dropping,” Sal said.

It was going to drop. The cage-lamp over the ring was hung on a crossbeam that had been sound last month. Last month was a long time in Nine-Below. The beam had a bloom on it now, same grey fur you got on anything that sat too near a bleed. You could pick it off with a nail if you wanted to watch the nail go the same way.

She set her boots on the rib of the duct and leaned out with the spare clamp. Cold came up through the steel and into her knees. Always cold here, even when the Pale sent down a thaw-week and the gutters ran like a river. The cold wasn’t weather. It was the sinks, pulling the heat out of things that had no business giving it up.

The fighter with the bad knee went down. Not a clean fall. His prosthetic locked, then powdered at the coupling, and he folded in a way legs are not meant to fold. The other one didn’t wait for a referee. There wasn’t one. He put a boot on the fallen lad’s good hip and leaned, just enough, and the crowd made that pleased ugly sound they kept for a finish.

The fallen lad still tried. You had to give him that. His fed arm — cheaper even than the knee, a cage of rod and hose taped into the biceps — spat a blue spit of light and then went dull. The smell came up through the grate, hot and sweet, like pennies on a stove. Somebody laughed. Somebody else shouted for the next bout like this one had been a delay.

Sal got the clamp on. The lamp steadied. She gave the bolt a look, the new one, and it was still a bolt. For now.

She climbed down the service ladder. The rungs left black on her palms. In the undercroft the air was thick with sweat and ozone and the sweet-rot smell of cheap implants working too hard. Donal had a desk that had been a door once. He sat on it with his bad hip cocked, counting markers.

“You saw that knee,” he said.

“I saw it.”

“Can you knock it loose so he doesn’t die on my drain?”

“I’m not a surgeon.”

“Didn’t ask you to be. Asked you to fetch a pin.”

Sal looked at the man on the boards. Young. Grey already at the mouth. The prosthetic was one of the pit specials — vacuum-fed, no governor, the sort you bought if you needed a win more than you needed a later. The coupling had gone to grit. You could see the original bone in there, or what was left of it, trying to remember it was a leg.

“He needs a clinic,” she said.

“He needs to not be on my floor,” Donal said. “Clinic’s a story people tell on the up-stacks. Pin. You want paying or not.”

She wanted paying.

The pin was in her roll, third pocket, wrapped in wax cloth so it wouldn’t catch the bleed. Stable brass, old stuff, pre-grid if you believed the lads who sold it. She didn’t believe much. She believed the brass stayed hard when the rest of a toolbox turned to flour.

She knelt. The fighter’s breath hitched. Up close he smelled like hot dust.

“This’ll hurt,” Sal said.

“Already does,” he said, which was fair.

She found the seizure point and drove the pin through the collar before the whole coupling could slough. There was a sound like wet sand packed in a fist. He made a noise and then bit it off. Blood and grey slurry ran over her wrist. She wiped it on the same thigh as the bolt.

“Get him off,” she told Donal. “And stop letting them run uncapped feeds. You’re cooking them.”

Donal smiled with half his mouth. “They ask for uncapped. I am a generous man.”

He paid her in three screws of brass and a chit for paste. The brass was good. She rolled it in the cloth and felt the weight of it, proper weight, the kind that didn’t lie. Three screws would keep the bench in bits for a week. It would not buy a way out of Nine-Below. It would not buy two ways out.

She didn’t say that. Donal wasn’t a wishing well.

“There’s more if you want it,” he said, counting still. “Chute work. Pale-side. I don’t send my own lads because my own lads come back talking.”

“I don’t do chutes.”

“You do what pays. Don’t get holy on me, Ward. Holy doesn’t keep a sister in paste.”

She looked at him then. Donal had a gift for finding the one sentence you didn’t want said in a room with other ears. The undercroft had plenty.

“Keep her out of your mouth,” Sal said.

He lifted both hands, markers and all. “Friendly concern. That’s all I’ve got. Friendly concern and a drain that backs up when men die on it.”

She left him the chit on the door-desk. Paste she could get without owing him a tone of voice.

On the stairs up, a kid was scratching a crown onto the wet plaster with a nail. The crown had an arm under it, silver, the way they always drew it. Somebody had tried to wash the last one off and only spread it. The King looked the same in every picture. No face worth mentioning. Just the arm, and the idea that if the arm held, the rest of you might.

Sal went past and did not comment. You didn’t comment on walls. Walls had a longer memory than mouths.

Outside, Sink Nine was doing its evening trick of looking almost liveable. Terraces sweating melt. Lights. A radio on a box that had never seen a grid. Laundry frozen stiff. A dog with no hair on its back legs, licking a puddle that steamed for no good reason.

Over it all, far enough to be a different country, the Pale.

You didn’t look at the Pale if you could help it. It sat on the high ice like a clean tooth in a bad mouth. White. Still. No bloom on those walls. No laundry. The air up there didn’t eat nails. People said that was because the King held the weather in his sleep. People said a lot.

Sal looked anyway, the once, because her neck did it without asking. Then she put her head down and took the ginnel home.

The ginnel ran between the back of third terrace and the still-house wall. It was wide enough for a barrow if you turned sideways and hated yourself. Melt dripped off a split pipe in a steady tick that nearly matched her metronome and then didn’t. Underfoot the duckboards had gone furred at the nails. She stepped the ones she trusted.

Mags from two doors down was sat on her own step with a basin in her lap, picking bloom off a door hinge with a butter knife. The hinge came away in flakes like fish skin. Mags didn’t look up.

“Your Tess after filters again?” Mags said.

“She’s after a lot of things.”

“Aren’t we all.” Mags held the knife out, the flake still on it, as if Sal might want a look. “This was new at thaw. You remember thaw. They said the bleed was moving off us.”

“They say that every thaw.”

“Aye.” Mags flicked the flake into the basin. It sat there in the water and didn’t dissolve, which was worse than if it had. “You hear eight-stack lost a walk last month.”

“I heard.”

“You hear they called it maintenance.”

“I heard that too.”

Mags finally looked at her. Eyes like she’d run out of surprise and was making do with tired. “You still doing Donal’s lamps?”

“When they drop.”

“Don’t let him put you on a chute. Chutes have a habit of knowing your name after.”

Sal almost said she’d already said no. She didn’t. Promises were just another thing that bloomed if you left them out.

Their place was a two-room off the third terrace, above the stills. The door stuck. The door always stuck. Inside, the metronome was going on the bench, tick-tick-tick, stubborn little bastard. She’d found it in a crate of ship-junk years back and never changed the weight. Digital clocks in Nine-Below ran fast or died. The metronome didn’t know there was a grid. It just kept time like time was still a thing you could agree on.

Tess was at the stove with her back to the door, stirring paste.

“You’re late,” Tess said.

“I’m paid.”

“That’s new.”

Sal dropped the roll on the bench and washed her hands in the basin. The water came brown, then clearer. The smear on her wrist didn’t want to leave. She used the nail brush until the skin stung.

“Pit job,” she said. “Knee went.”

Tess glanced over. She was younger by six years and looked it less and less. Hair tied up with a bit of flex. Burn mark on her forearm from the stills. Her hands were wrapped in clean rag, which meant they’d been bad today.

“You eat?” Tess said.

“I will.”

“That’s not a yes.”

Sal took the bowl she was given. Algae paste with a bit of krill if you were lucky. Tonight they were lucky, or Tess had held some back. It tasted of salt and green and the tin it came in. She ate it standing up, which Tess hated, which was why she did it.

“There’s talk of tickets,” Tess said, like she was mentioning weather.

Sal kept eating.

“Not Pale tickets. Don’t be thick. Work tickets. Thaw-line. They want hands on the new soil.”

“They always want hands,” Sal said. “Hands are cheap.”

“It’s soil, Sal. Actual dirt. Not this.” Tess tapped the bowl with her knuckle. The rag made a dull sound. “You plant something in that ground and it comes up like it used to. That’s what they’re saying.”

“Who’s they.”

“Pex. And the women on fourth.”

“Pex would say there was a pub on the moon if it got him a lift.”

Tess turned fully then. The rag on her left hand had a spot coming through, grey, not blood.

“I’m not asking you to believe Pex. I’m asking you to look at me and not do that face.”

“What face.”

“The one where you’ve already decided we’re leaving and I’m a problem you have to pack.”

Sal set the bowl down harder than she meant. The metronome ticked through it.

“I haven’t decided owt.”

“You stand at that window every night and look at the Pale like it’s a bus you’re going to catch.”

“I look at it because it’s there. Same as you look at dirt that isn’t ours.”

Tess laughed, short. “There it is.”

They let it sit. The stills below thumped, a slow ugly heartbeat. Somewhere two stacks over a siren tried to start and thought better of it.

Sal unwrapped the brass and laid the screws in the tin she kept under the bench, behind the false back. Tess watched. She always watched the tin. She never asked how much was in it. That was the deal they hadn’t said out loud: Sal would try to buy them off this terrace, and Tess would pretend the stills were a life, and neither of them would call the other a fool until they had to.

“Let me see your hands,” Sal said.

“They’re fine.”

“They’re not.”

Tess peeled the rag back like it was a dare. The knuckles were wrong. Not swollen. Thinned. A bloom under the skin, the same grey you got on a beam when the bleed had been at it. You could still bend them. That was the cruel bit. They worked. They just wouldn’t keep working.

Sal didn’t touch. Touching didn’t help.

“You need to stay off the vats,” she said.

“Do I. And who runs them. You, between pit jobs.”

“I’ll find a filter.”

“You’ve found four filters. They last a week and then they go the same as the last ones.” Tess wrapped the hand again, neat, professional. She’d learned that from watching Sal wrap tools. “It’s the air, not the vats. You know it. The whole stack’s on a bleed. We can change rags or we can go where the air isn’t hungry.”

“The thaw-line is still on the grid,” Sal said. “They’ll siphon there same as here. They just hide it under trees.”

“You haven’t seen the trees.”

“Neither have you.”

Tess’s mouth went small. She picked up the spoon again.

Sal sat at the bench and wound the metronome. It didn’t need winding. She did it because her fingers wanted a job that didn’t involve anybody’s knee.

Through the window the Pale was a white edge. No lights you could count. They didn’t waste them on being looked at.

She thought about the three screws. About tickets that weren’t tickets. About a knee that had been a knee that morning.

She thought, and this was the bit she did not say to Tess, that if you kept your head down and your tools dry and you didn’t owe the wrong people, the weather went over you. It went over other terraces. That was the rule she’d lived on since their dad stepped out for a part and didn’t come back in the same shape he’d left.

Keep off the grid. Don’t be interesting. Let the King hold whatever he was holding.

The metronome agreed with her, or anyway it didn’t argue.
